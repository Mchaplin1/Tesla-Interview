Before It Ships: Catching AI Hallucinations in Training Documents

This course teaches one thing: how to catch AI hallucinations in a training document before it ships.

What's a hallucination? It's when AI states something false or made up as if it were true. In training documents, it often looks like a detail nobody gave it, a missing step, or a fact turned upside down.

By Maya Chaplin · November 2026

Start the story Go to the practice

Who this is for

Fellow instructional designers who are new to catching AI hallucinations

You use AI tools, or you're starting to, to turn notes from experts into training documents. You may not have a routine yet for checking what the AI gives back. You don't need any technical background to take this.

  • You've built training before, but checking AI writing is new to you.
  • You want a simple routine you can use on your next document.
  • You'll leave with a one-minute checklist to keep.

Course objective:

Given an AI-written training document and the expert's original notes, you will catch every hallucination and decide whether the document is ready to share.

How you'll get there

  1. UnderstandBloom's level 2

    Classify each hallucination as made up, left out, or says the opposite.

    Learned in Part 2, The mistakes. Checked when you tag each line in the practice.

  2. AnalyzeBloom's level 4

    Compare the document with the notes, line by line, and flag each hallucination.

    Practiced in Part 5, The test.

  3. EvaluateBloom's level 5

    Decide whether the document is ready to share, and justify your call in your own words.

    Done at the end of Part 5, The call.

Part 2 · The mistakes Define

"It looks finished. That's the problem."

Rosa's questionWhat exactly goes wrong, and what problem are we solving?

M
"My first month using AI, I sent out a document that looked perfect. It had a supply code in it that didn't exist. People spent a week ordering the wrong thing. The writing was great. It was just wrong."Marcus, Rosa's manager

AI rarely makes sloppy mistakes. It makes confident ones that sound right. These are hallucinations, and Marcus names the three kinds he sees most often.

Made up

It adds something the notes never said

Codes, numbers, or extra "helpful" steps. When the notes leave a gap, AI fills it with something that sounds official.

Use cleaning spray #CS-90.

Left out or out of order

It drops a step or shuffles the order

Often a safety step, because AI treats it like a detail it can trim. Ask what should come before each step, not just whether it's there.

1. Turn the machine off.
2. Reach inside.

Says the opposite

It flips what the notes said

A number, a direction, or a condition changed to whatever sounded more normal to the AI.

Put it in face down.
Notes said: face up

The problem Rosa defines

How might I make sure an AI-written training document matches what the expert said, before a new person follows it on their first morning?

Part 4 · The checklist Prototype

5:00 p.m. A checklist on a sticky note

Rosa's questionWhat's the quickest version we can try right now?

Rosa doesn't need a perfect process. She needs something she can try in the next five minutes. She writes a first version on a sticky note and puts it on her monitor.

The one-minute check

Version 1
  1. Read the notes first. Know what the expert said before you see what the AI did with it.
  2. Find every detail in the notes. Numbers, codes, names, extra steps. If you can't point to it, cut it or ask.
  3. Walk the steps in order. Match each one to the notes. Look hardest at the first steps, where one small slip ruins everything after.

A prototype is only useful if you test it. So Rosa tries it on the real document.

    Part 6 · The next morning Iterate

    Tuesday, 7:00 a.m.

    Rosa's questionWhat did testing teach us, and what do we change?

    Rosa fixed the three lines, sent Dee a quick message to confirm, and had a yes by 5:40. The next morning, the new hires made a perfect pot of coffee without a single question.

    But testing taught Rosa something. The missing filter was the hardest to spot, because you can't point at a line that isn't there. The clue was the last step: "throw out the used filter," when no filter was ever put in. So she updated her sticky note.

    The one-minute check

    Version 2 · yours to keep
    1. Read the notes first. Know what the expert said before you see what the AI did with it.
    2. Find every detail in the notes. Numbers, codes, names, extra steps. If you can't point to it, cut it or ask.
    3. Walk the steps in order. Match each one to the notes. Look hardest at the first steps, where one small slip ruins everything after.
    4. Look for steps that undo something. "Throw out the filter," "turn it off," "put it back." If nothing earlier set that up, a step is missing. Added after testing

    Next time, she'll test version 2 and keep improving it. That's the point: the checklist is never finished, and neither is the person using it.

    For the hiring team

    Designer's notes

    The decisions behind this course, in brief.

    What it teaches

    One thing: how to catch AI hallucinations in a training document before it ships.

    How

    A 12-minute, self-paced story with one hands-on practice and one decision.

    Why this way

    The skill is careful, individual reading. Self-paced fits that, and the time and budget.

    Why I chose this topic

    AI helps us. It doesn't replace us.

    I often see people trust what AI gives them and ship it without checking. AI writing looks clean and confident, and deadlines are real.

    I use AI every day, and it makes me faster. But I feel strongly that the human part can't be forgotten. AI can draft. People have to decide.

    Part 1

    What shaped the design

    Who

    Target audience

    Fellow instructional designers who are beginners at catching AI hallucinations. They know how to build training, use AI on tight deadlines, and work on different teams and schedules.

    What

    Format

    A self-paced web module, about 12 minutes. One story, one practice, one decision, and a checklist to keep.

    Why

    Why self-paced

    Self-paced compared with instructor-led training
    FactorSelf-paced (chosen)Instructor-led
    Time12 minutes, whenever it fitsAn hour or more, at a set time
    SchedulingNoneOne time across many calendars and time zones
    Cost to runClose to nothing on free hosting or an existing platformFacilitator hours, a room or video platform, and everyone off work at once
    New hiresSend the linkRun another session
    UpdatesEdit one fileUpdate materials and retrain facilitators

    Trade-off: no live discussion. If budget allowed, I'd add an optional 15-minute team huddle afterward.

    Part 3

    Key design decisions

    01

    Design thinking, behind the scenes

    The six parts follow Empathize, Define, Ideate, Prototype, Test, and Iterate. Each part is labeled by the story, with the stage as a small tag, so the course teaches one thing while the process stays visible.

    02

    A story, not a lecture

    Rosa is new, busy, and about to hit send, which is where the learner will be. Marcus models the habit, and the learner takes Rosa's chair for the practice.

    03

    Practice that copies the real task

    • Notes and AI document side by side: eight short lines, three hallucinations, one of each kind.
    • An everyday task, so reviewers and learners spend their attention on the skill, not the subject.
    • A hidden clue: the last step throws out a filter that was never added. Part 6 turns it into a new checklist item.
    • Feedback says what would happen to a real person, not just "wrong."
    04

    Visuals only where they help

    A progress tracker, cast cards, and one labeled coffee maker drawing, the only visual that teaches. No stock photos.

    05

    What I cut

    How AI works, video and voiceover, and a passing score. None of them help someone do the task.

    06

    AI's role, and measuring success

    I used AI to draft the story and suggest hallucinations, then chose and rewrote them by hand and checked every line of feedback against Dee's notes.

    Success means more hallucinations caught on a new document a week later, fewer reaching published training, and fewer corrections from experts.

    For the hiring team

    Accessibility

    How this course is built to work for everyone, and what I'd do before launch.

    The goal

    One version that works for everyone

    No one should be sent to a "separate" course. This version is designed toward WCAG 2.1 AA, the standard ADA compliance is usually measured against. It hasn't been tested with people who use assistive technology yet, so the steps below come before launch.

    Part 1

    Built into this version

    Keyboard

    Everything works with a keyboard alone, with a clear outline on whatever has focus, plus a "Skip to the practice" link.

    More than color

    Color is never the only signal. Flags and feedback also use words and icons.

    Screen readers

    The coffee maker drawing has a written description. Decorative icons are hidden. Feedback is announced as it appears.

    Contrast and zoom

    Text contrast meets AA. The layout stacks into one column on a phone or when zoomed in.

    Calm by default

    No timers and no sound that plays on its own. Motion is reduced for people who ask their device for less.

    Plain language

    Everyday words and an everyday task, so nothing gets in the way of the skill.

    Part 2

    Steps before launch

    1. Run an automated check

      Fix everything it flags.

    2. Test by hand

      Go through the whole course with a keyboard only, and with the screen readers on Windows and on Apple devices.

    3. Zoom in

      Check at 200% and 400% that nothing is cut off or overlaps.

    4. Test with real people

      Ask two or three people who use assistive technology every day to take the course, and watch where they get stuck.

    5. Publish a statement

      Say what was tested and how to ask for another format.

    Part 3

    If an alternate version is needed

    If testing shows the practice is harder for some learners, I'd add an alternate version. Here's what would be different.

    01

    Practice as a simple form

    Each line gets one dropdown ("Looks fine," "Made up," "Left out or out of order," "Says the opposite") in place of the select-then-tag steps.

    02

    Notes above the document

    Screen reader and zoom users read one thing at a time, so stacking is easier to follow than side by side.

    03

    Matching step numbers

    Each AI line would name the step in Dee's notes it should match.

    04

    Audio with a transcript

    An optional narrated version of the story, with a written transcript.

    05

    A printable version

    Large-print and high-contrast, with answers on a separate page.

    06

    Same objectives, same story

    Only the way you interact changes, so everyone builds the same skill.
